14  DiD with Continuous Treatment

The DiD chapter treats the treatment as on or off. Many treatments come in amounts. A county gets one Walmart or five. A state raises the minimum wage by fifty cents or by three dollars. A hospital receives a small grant or a large one. With a dose we want more than the effect of being treated. We want to know how the effect changes with the dose.

We first look at two periods, where Callaway, Goodman-Bacon, and Sant’Anna (2024), CGBS from here on, show what parallel trends identifies with a dose and what it leaves out. Two assumptions carry everything that follows: parallel trends and strong parallel trends. We then move to staggered adoption, using the heterogeneous-slopes extension of extended two-way fixed effects (ETWFE) in Wooldridge (2026), and replicate his study of Walmart and county retail employment.

14.1 Two periods

We observe units \(i = 1, \dots, n\) in two periods, \(t = 1, 2\). Nobody is treated in period 1. Before period 2, each unit receives a dose \(D_i \ge 0\), and units with \(D_i = 0\) stay untreated. For each dose \(d\), unit \(i\) has a potential outcome \(Y_{it}(d)\), and we observe \(Y_{it} = Y_{it}(D_i)\). No anticipation means \(Y_{i1}(d) = Y_{i1}(0)\) for every \(d\). Let \(\Delta Y_i = Y_{i2} - Y_{i1}\).

There are two natural estimands. Take two doses \(d\) and \(d'\). The first estimand, \(ATT(d \mid d')\), is the average effect of dose \(d\), relative to no treatment, among the units that actually received dose \(d'\):

\[ATT(d \mid d') = E[Y_{i2}(d) - Y_{i2}(0) \mid D_i = d']. \tag{14.1}\]

The leading case is \(ATT(d \mid d)\), the effect of each group’s own dose. The second estimand, \(ATE(d)\), is the effect of dose \(d\) averaged over all treated units, whatever dose they received:

\[ATE(d) = E[Y_{i2}(d) - Y_{i2}(0) \mid D_i > 0]. \tag{14.2}\]

Its derivative \(ATE'(d)\) is the average causal response at \(d\): the average effect of a small increase in the dose.

14.1.1 Parallel trends

The first assumption is parallel trends. With a dose it says that, without treatment, every dose group would have followed the trend of the untreated:

\[E[Y_{i2}(0) - Y_{i1}(0) \mid D_i = d] = E[Y_{i2}(0) - Y_{i1}(0) \mid D_i = 0] \quad \text{for all } d. \tag{14.3}\]

In words, the change in the untreated potential outcome between periods 1 and 2 is the same at every dose, including zero. The assumption restricts untreated outcomes only. Under Equation 14.3 and no anticipation,

\[E[\Delta Y_i \mid D_i = d] - E[\Delta Y_i \mid D_i = 0] = ATT(d \mid d). \tag{14.4}\]

This is the binary DiD done separately at each dose. Parallel trends identifies the whole curve \(ATT(d \mid d)\), each dose group’s effect of its own dose. With more periods before treatment we can check it in the usual way: the dose groups should have moved together before anyone was treated.

Parallel trends does not identify comparisons across doses. For the two doses \(d\) and \(d'\),

\[ATT(d \mid d) - ATT(d' \mid d') = \underbrace{ATT(d \mid d) - ATT(d' \mid d)}_{\text{causal response}} + \underbrace{ATT(d' \mid d) - ATT(d' \mid d')}_{\text{selection on gains}}. \tag{14.5}\]

The first term is causal. It is what moving the dose from \(d'\) to \(d\) does for the units that chose \(d\). The second term compares two groups at the same dose \(d'\). It is non-zero when the units that chose \(d\) would gain differently from dose \(d'\) than the units that chose \(d'\). Parallel trends says nothing about this term, and pre-treatment data cannot reveal it, because it involves treated outcomes. So the slope of the curve in Equation 14.4 mixes the causal response with selection on gains.

14.1.2 Strong parallel trends

The second assumption removes the selection term. CGBS call it strong parallel trends:

\[E[Y_{i2}(d) - Y_{i1}(0) \mid D_i = d] = E[Y_{i2}(d) - Y_{i1}(0) \mid D_i > 0] \quad \text{for all } d > 0. \tag{14.6}\]

In words, the average path from untreated in period 1 to dose \(d\) in period 2 is the same for the units that chose \(d\) as for all treated units. Together with Equation 14.3 it gives \(ATT(d \mid d) = ATE(d)\) for every \(d\): the average effect of a dose is the same in every dose group, whatever dose the group chose. The selection term in Equation 14.5 is then zero. Comparisons across doses are causal, and the slope of the identified curve is the average causal response.

Strong parallel trends is a stronger assumption than parallel trends, and of a different kind. It restricts treated potential outcomes, so no pre-treatment data can check it, and passing a pre-trend test says nothing about it. It restricts how units choose their dose. It is plausible when the dose is set for reasons unrelated to the gain, for example by a formula or a lottery. It is hard to defend when units choose their dose knowing what they will gain.

Wooldridge (2026) reaches the same point from a regression. Let \(B_i\) be the gain of unit \(i\) from one unit of dose, so that the effect is linear in the dose with a slope that varies across units: \(Y_{i2}(d) = Y_{i2}(0) + d B_i\). Let \(\gamma = E[Y_{i2}(0) - Y_{i1}(0)]\) be the untreated trend, and let \(\delta = E(B_i \mid D_i > 0)\) be the average slope among the treated. Under Equation 14.3,

\[E(\Delta Y_i \mid D_i) = \gamma + \delta D_i \tag{14.7}\]

holds when \(E(B_i \mid D_i = d) = \delta\) for every \(d > 0\). That condition is strong parallel trends for the linear model. Adding powers of \(D_i\) to the regression relaxes the linearity.

14.2 Staggered adoption

Now units enter treatment at different times, as in the ETWFE section of the DiD chapter. Periods run \(t = 1, \dots, T\), and the first period in which any unit is treated is \(q\). A cohort \(g \in \{q, \dots, T\}\) is the set of units first treated in period \(g\). Let \(D_{ig} = 1\) if unit \(i\) is in cohort \(g\), and \(D_{ig} = 0\) otherwise. Never-treated units have \(D_{ig} = 0\) for every \(g\). The vector \(\mathbf D_i = (D_{iq}, \dots, D_{iT})\) collects unit \(i\)’s cohort dummies. For each period \(s\), the time dummy \(fs_t\) equals 1 if \(t = s\) and 0 otherwise.

The treatment \(\mathbf W_{it}\) is a \(1 \times J\) row vector, where \(J\) is the number of treatment variables. A single dose has \(J = 1\). A dose and its square has \(J = 2\). A set of mutually exclusive treatment levels has \(J\) equal to the number of levels. Every unit has \(\mathbf W_{it} = \mathbf 0\) before its entry period, and \(\mathbf W_i\) stacks unit \(i\)’s rows over all \(T\) periods. We write \(\mathbf w\) for a possible value of \(\mathbf W_{it}\). The dose may change after entry. In the Walmart data it is the number of stores open.

Wooldridge (2026) writes potential outcomes as linear in the treatment, with slopes that vary by unit and period. Let \(\mathbf B_{it}\) be a \(J \times 1\) vector of random slopes, whose \(j\)-th element is unit \(i\)’s gain in period \(t\) from one unit of treatment variable \(j\). Let \(\gamma_t\) be a time effect, \(C_i\) an unobserved unit effect, and \(U_{it}\) an idiosyncratic error. The potential outcome at treatment value \(\mathbf w\) is

\[Y_{it}(\mathbf w) = \mathbf w\, \mathbf B_{it} + \gamma_t + C_i + U_{it}. \tag{14.8}\]

For a single dose, \(J = 1\), write the scalar dose as \(w\). Subtracting the untreated potential outcome cancels the time effect, the unit effect and the error:

\[Y_{it}(w) - Y_{it}(0) = w\, B_{it}. \tag{14.9}\]

So \(B_{it}\) is the difference in potential outcomes per unit of dose. With several treatment variables, the \(j\)-th element of \(\mathbf B_{it}\) is the change in the potential outcome from one more unit of treatment variable \(j\), holding the others fixed. The additive \(\gamma_t + C_i\) is parallel trends. Nothing restricts \(\mathbf B_{it}\). The estimand is its mean for each cohort and period:

\[\boldsymbol\delta_{gs} = E(\mathbf B_{is} \mid D_{ig} = 1), \qquad s \ge g. \tag{14.10}\]

In words, \(\boldsymbol\delta_{gs}\) is the average effect per unit of each treatment variable for cohort \(g\) in period \(s\). Because the potential outcome is linear in the dose, it is also the effect of one more unit at any dose: going from 0 to 1 has the same effect as going from 1 to 2. Adding powers of the dose, or dummies for dose levels, to \(\mathbf W_{it}\) relaxes this. With a binary treatment, \(\boldsymbol\delta_{gs}\) is the cohort-period ATT of ETWFE.

Wooldridge assumes no anticipation and strict exogeneity. Strict exogeneity says the error \(U_{it}\) has mean zero given the whole treatment path \(\mathbf W_i\), the unit effect \(C_i\) and the slopes \(\mathbf B_{it}\). He needs one more assumption, which has no counterpart in the binary case. He calls it limited selection:

\[E(\mathbf B_{it} \mid \mathbf W_i) = E(\mathbf B_{it} \mid \mathbf D_i). \tag{14.11}\]

Once we know when a unit entered, its dose path carries no information about its average gain. With a binary treatment the entry date fixes the whole treatment path, so Equation 14.11 holds automatically. With two periods and a scalar dose it is the strong parallel trends of Equation 14.6 in its linear form. Parallel trends enters through the additive \(\gamma_t + C_i\) in Equation 14.8. So the staggered estimates rest on the same two assumptions as the two-period ones.

Let \(A_i = E(C_i \mid \mathbf W_i)\) be the mean of the unit effect given the treatment path. Under these assumptions,

\[E(Y_{it} \mid \mathbf W_i) = \sum_{g=q}^{T} \sum_{s=g}^{T} (D_{ig}\, fs_t\, \mathbf W_{it})\, \boldsymbol\delta_{gs} + \gamma_t + A_i. \tag{14.12}\]

\(A_i\) is a unit effect. So a two-way fixed effects (TWFE) regression of \(Y_{it}\) on the interactions \(D_{ig}\, fs_t\, \mathbf W_{it}\), with unit and time effects, estimates every \(\boldsymbol\delta_{gs}\). This is ETWFE with the treatment indicator replaced by the dose. Standard errors are clustered by unit.

There are many cohort-period slopes, so we aggregate them. Let \(e\) be the exposure time, the number of periods since entry, and let \(N_g\) be the number of units in cohort \(g\). Let \(\hat{\boldsymbol\delta}_{gs}\) be the TWFE estimate of \(\boldsymbol\delta_{gs}\). The estimated effect at exposure \(e\), written \(\hat{\boldsymbol\tau}_{e}\), averages over the cohorts observed that long, with weights proportional to cohort size:

\[\hat{\boldsymbol\tau}_{e} = \sum_{g=q}^{T-e} \frac{N_g}{N_q + \dots + N_{T-e}}\, \hat{\boldsymbol\delta}_{g,g+e}. \tag{14.13}\]

The overall effect weights every cohort-period slope by \(N_g\). In a balanced panel it is the average slope over all treated unit-periods.

Pre-trends can be tested in two ways. A lead is a dummy \(D_{ig}\, fs_t\) for a period \(s\) before cohort \(g\) enters. The first test fits TWFE on the untreated observations only, the unit-periods with \(\mathbf W_{it} = \mathbf 0\), with leads for \(s \le g - 2\), so that the period just before entry is the base. It then tests whether the leads are zero. The second test adds the same leads to Equation 14.12. With a binary treatment the two give identical estimates. With a dose they differ. Both tests check parallel trends. Neither checks limited selection. When the leads show a trend, Wooldridge adds cohort-specific linear trends to Equation 14.12. For each cohort \(g\), the regressor \(D_{ig} \cdot t\) is a linear time trend for that cohort only. The assumption then becomes that each cohort’s gap from the never-treated units would have kept growing along the same line after entry.

14.3 Wooldridge’s regression and the CGBS estimator

The two approaches target the same objects. Under parallel trends and strong parallel trends, each identifies the average effect of the dose for every cohort and period. They differ in how they estimate it and in what they can handle.

The first difference is the comparison group. Wooldridge runs one TWFE regression in levels on all periods, Equation 14.12. Every untreated observation serves as a control: the never-treated units, the not-yet-treated units, and the pre-entry periods of every cohort. In the binary case this regression is the imputation estimator of Borusyak, Jaravel, and Spiess (2024). Callaway, Goodman-Bacon, and Sant’Anna (2024) build on Callaway and Sant’Anna (2021) instead. For each cohort and period they compare the cohort with the never-treated or the not-yet-treated units, using the long difference from the period just before entry, and fit the dose response in that comparison. The regression uses more data, and it is more efficient when the errors are homoskedastic and serially uncorrelated. In exchange it needs parallel trends in every pre-entry period. The differencing approach needs it only from the base period on.

The second difference is the shape of the dose response. In Wooldridge’s regression, the shape comes from the functions of the dose placed in \(\mathbf W_{it}\): the dose itself, its powers, or dummies for dose levels. The estimand is the average slope \(\boldsymbol\delta_{gs}\). CGBS fit a B-spline in the dose within each cohort and period, or a nonparametric curve whose complexity is chosen from the data. They report the curve \(ATT(d \mid d)\) and its derivative. The two also treat a dose near zero differently. With two periods, Wooldridge’s regression has no separate intercept for the treated units, so its fitted effect goes to zero as the dose goes to zero. The CGBS fit gives the treated units their own intercept, so the effect can jump at any positive dose. The two coincide once a dummy for a positive dose is added to \(\mathbf W_{it}\).

The third difference is scope. The table compares Wooldridge’s regression with the R package contdid, version 0.1.1, which implements CGBS.

14.4 Walmart and county retail employment

Wooldridge (2026) applies the method to the county panel of Brown and Butts (2025). It covers 1,288 counties from 1977 to 1999. No county had a Walmart before 1986. Stores then opened in 14 cohorts, from 1986 to 1999, and 395 counties never got one. The treatment is a single dose, so \(J = 1\) and we write it without bold: \(W_{it}\) is the number of Walmarts open in county \(i\) in year \(t\). The outcome is log retail employment.

One more Walmart raises retail employment by 3.4%, with a standard error of 0.57 percentage points. In the binary case this regression makes forbidden comparisons, which use already-treated units as controls, when effects vary by cohort and time. The same holds with a dose. So we build the regressors of Equation 14.12, one for each cohort and year from entry on: \(W_{it}\) times the cohort dummy times the year dummy.

Code 
cohorts = 1986:1999
cells = [(g, s) for g in cohorts for s in cohorts if s >= g]
wcols = [Symbol("w_$(g)_$(s)") for (g, s) in cells]
for ((g, s), c) in zip(cells, wcols)
    wm[!, c] = (wm.cohort .== g) .* (wm.year .== s) .* wm.n_open
end
trcols = [Symbol("tr_$g") for g in cohorts]
for (g, c) in zip(cohorts, trcols)
    wm[!, c] = (wm.cohort .== g) .* wm.year
end

fit_het = feols(wm, term(:log_retail_emp) ~ sum(term.(wcols)) + fe(:fips) + fe(:year),
                vcov = Vcov.cluster(:fips))
length(coef(fit_het))
105

That gives 105 slopes. We aggregate them with Equation 14.13. Each aggregate is a linear combination of the slopes with fixed weights, so its standard error comes from the clustered covariance matrix. This matches Stata’s margins in Wooldridge’s code.

Code 
Ng = Dict(g => sum((wm.year .== 1999) .& (wm.cohort .== g)) for g in cohorts)

function aggregate_slopes(fit)
    pos = Dict(n => i for (i, n) in enumerate(coefnames(fit)))
    b = coef(fit); V = vcov(fit)
    lincomb(a) = (dot(a, b), sqrt(a' * V * a))
    out = map(0:13) do e
        gs = [g for g in cohorts if g + e <= 1999]
        a = zeros(length(b))
        for g in gs
            a[pos["w_$(g)_$(g + e)"]] = Ng[g] / sum(Ng[h] for h in gs)
        end
        lincomb(a)
    end
    a = zeros(length(b))
    for (g, s) in cells
        a[pos["w_$(g)_$(s)"]] = Ng[g]
    end
    push!(out, lincomb(a ./ sum(a)))
end
agg_het = aggregate_slopes(fit_het);

Before reading the effects, we check pre-trends. Following the first test above, we fit TWFE on the untreated county-years with a lead for each cohort and each year at least two years before entry. We then average the leads by years until entry, with the cohort weights of Equation 14.13.

Code 
un = wm[wm.n_open .== 0, [:fips, :year, :cohort, :log_retail_emp]]
leads = [(g, s) for g in cohorts for s in 1977:1999 if s <= g - 2]
lcols = [Symbol("l_$(g)_$(s)") for (g, s) in leads]
for ((g, s), c) in zip(leads, lcols)
    un[!, c] = Float64.((un.cohort .== g) .& (un.year .== s))
end
fit_pre = feols(un, term(:log_retail_emp) ~ sum(term.(lcols)) + fe(:fips) + fe(:year),
                vcov = Vcov.cluster(:fips))
pos = Dict(n => i for (i, n) in enumerate(coefnames(fit_pre)))
b = coef(fit_pre); V = vcov(fit_pre)
pre = DataFrame(map(2:22) do k
    gs = [g for g in cohorts if g - k >= 1977]
    a = zeros(length(b))
    for g in gs
        a[pos["l_$(g)_$(g - k)"]] = Ng[g] / sum(Ng[h] for h in gs)
    end
    (time = -k, estimate = dot(a, b), se = sqrt(a' * V * a))
end)

The table reproduces Table 7.1 of Wooldridge (2026), estimates and standard errors, to all four decimals.

Without trends, one more store raises retail employment by about 3% to 4% in the first years. The effect climbs to 9% by year 13. The overall effect is 0.0386 (0.0051). It is close to the single-coefficient TWFE estimate of 0.0344, and a little more precise.

The cohort trends change the estimates a lot. The effect in the entry year is 0.0049 (0.0033), indistinguishable from zero. From the entry year to year 6, the effects are between a sixth and a half of their values without trends. They then rise steadily, and by year 13 the two columns agree at about 0.09. The overall effect falls to 0.0156 (0.0046), less than half the estimate without trends. The trends also make the estimates more precise up to about year 8. The long exposures rest on the earliest cohorts only, so their standard errors grow. Year 13 comes from the 1986 cohort alone.

For comparison, we add the same 14 cohort trends to the single-coefficient regression. One slope on the store count is then shared by every cohort and year.

Effect of one Walmart on log retail employment in the 1990 cohort, by year since entry, with 95% intervals.

Without trends, one store raises retail employment in the 1990 cohort by 1.7% in the entry year and by 4.0% a year later, and the effect then settles near 2.7%. From year 1 on, the estimates are about twice their standard errors or more. With cohort trends the path is lower, and the gap grows from 0.6 percentage points in the entry year to 1.6 by year 9. Only the year-1 estimate, 3.1%, is more than twice its standard error. The growing gap is what we would see if the cohort trend, which is also fit on post-entry years, absorbs part of an effect that grows with exposure. The plot holds the dose at one store. The cohort’s average number of stores rises from 1.08 in 1990 to 1.75 in 1999, so the effect it actually experienced grows faster than the plotted per-store effect.

14.5 Summary

Wooldridge’s method is ETWFE with the treatment indicator replaced by the dose. The regression in Equation 14.12 interacts cohort, year and dose. Each interaction has its own slope \(\boldsymbol\delta_{gs}\), the average effect of one more unit of dose for cohort \(g\) in year \(s\), and the slopes are averaged by exposure time. Covariates, cohort trends, exit and unbalanced panels enter as extra terms in the same regression.

Two assumptions identify the slopes. Parallel trends lets us compare each cohort’s change with the change of the untreated units, and the pre-trend tests check it. Strong parallel trends, which Wooldridge’s limited selection extends to staggered adoption, says the dose a unit ends up with tells us nothing about its average gain. It makes comparisons across doses causal, and no test in the data checks it. In the Walmart data parallel trends fails, so we add cohort trends, and the overall effect of one more store falls from 3.9% to 1.6%.
