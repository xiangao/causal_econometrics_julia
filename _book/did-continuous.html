14  DiD with Continuous Treatment

The DiD chapter treats the treatment as on or off. Many treatments come in amounts. A county gets one Walmart or five. A state raises the minimum wage by fifty cents or by three dollars. A hospital receives a small grant or a large one. With a dose we want more than the effect of being treated. We want to know how the effect changes with the dose.

We first look at two periods, where Callaway, Goodman-Bacon, and Sant’Anna (2024) show what parallel trends identifies with a dose and what it leaves out. A simulation with known truth shows the difference. We then move to staggered adoption, using the heterogeneous-slopes extension of extended two-way fixed effects (ETWFE) in Wooldridge (2026), and replicate his study of Walmart and county retail employment.

14.1 Two periods

We observe units \(i = 1, \dots, n\) in two periods, \(t = 1, 2\). Nobody is treated in period 1. Before period 2, each unit receives a dose \(D_i \ge 0\), and units with \(D_i = 0\) stay untreated. For each dose \(d\), unit \(i\) has a potential outcome \(Y_{it}(d)\), and we observe \(Y_{it} = Y_{it}(D_i)\). No anticipation means \(Y_{i1}(d) = Y_{i1}(0)\) for every \(d\). Let \(\Delta Y_i = Y_{i2} - Y_{i1}\).

There are two natural estimands. Take two doses \(d\) and \(d'\). The first estimand, \(ATT(d \mid d')\), is the average effect of dose \(d\), relative to no treatment, among the units that actually received dose \(d'\):

\[ATT(d \mid d') = E[Y_{i2}(d) - Y_{i2}(0) \mid D_i = d']. \tag{14.1}\]

The leading case is \(ATT(d \mid d)\), the effect of each group’s own dose. The second estimand, \(ATE(d)\), is the effect of dose \(d\) averaged over all treated units, whatever dose they received:

\[ATE(d) = E[Y_{i2}(d) - Y_{i2}(0) \mid D_i > 0]. \tag{14.2}\]

Its derivative \(ATE'(d)\) is the average causal response at \(d\): the average effect of a small increase in the dose.

Parallel trends with a dose says that, without treatment, every dose group would have followed the trend of the untreated:

\[E[Y_{i2}(0) - Y_{i1}(0) \mid D_i = d] = E[Y_{i2}(0) - Y_{i1}(0) \mid D_i = 0] \quad \text{for all } d. \tag{14.3}\]

Under Equation 14.3 and no anticipation,

\[E[\Delta Y_i \mid D_i = d] - E[\Delta Y_i \mid D_i = 0] = ATT(d \mid d). \tag{14.4}\]

This is the binary DiD done separately at each dose. It identifies the whole curve \(ATT(d \mid d)\).

The difficulty starts when we compare doses. For the two doses \(d\) and \(d'\),

\[ATT(d \mid d) - ATT(d' \mid d') = \underbrace{ATT(d \mid d) - ATT(d' \mid d)}_{\text{causal response}} + \underbrace{ATT(d' \mid d) - ATT(d' \mid d')}_{\text{selection on gains}}. \tag{14.5}\]

The first term is causal. It is what moving the dose from \(d'\) to \(d\) does for the units that chose \(d\). The second term compares two groups at the same dose \(d'\). It is non-zero when the units that chose \(d\) would gain differently from dose \(d'\) than the units that chose \(d'\). Parallel trends says nothing about this term. So the slope of the curve in Equation 14.4 mixes the causal response with selection on gains.

Callaway, Goodman-Bacon, and Sant’Anna (2024) remove the second term with strong parallel trends:

\[E[Y_{i2}(d) - Y_{i1}(0) \mid D_i = d] = E[Y_{i2}(d) - Y_{i1}(0) \mid D_i > 0] \quad \text{for all } d > 0. \tag{14.6}\]

In words, the average path from untreated in period 1 to dose \(d\) in period 2 is the same for the units that chose \(d\) as for all treated units. Together with Equation 14.3 it gives \(ATT(d \mid d) = ATE(d)\) for every \(d\). Comparisons across doses are then causal, and the slope of the identified curve is the average causal response.

Wooldridge (2026) reaches the same point from a regression. Let \(B_i\) be the gain of unit \(i\) from one unit of dose, so that the effect is linear in the dose with a slope that varies across units: \(Y_{i2}(d) = Y_{i2}(0) + d B_i\). Let \(\gamma = E[Y_{i2}(0) - Y_{i1}(0)]\) be the untreated trend, and let \(\delta = E(B_i \mid D_i > 0)\) be the average slope among the treated. Under Equation 14.3,

\[E(\Delta Y_i \mid D_i) = \gamma + \delta D_i \tag{14.7}\]

holds when \(E(B_i \mid D_i = d) = \delta\) for every \(d > 0\). That condition is strong parallel trends for the linear model. Adding powers of \(D_i\) to the regression relaxes the linearity.

14.2 A simulation with known truth

We now check these claims on data where we know the truth. The aim is to see which curve the DiD estimator in Equation 14.4 recovers when gains are, and are not, related to the dose.

Each scenario has 3,000 units observed in two periods. 1,000 units are untreated. The other 2,000 receive a dose drawn uniformly on \([0.5, 2.5]\). The untreated outcome is the sum of a unit effect \(\alpha_i\), a period effect \(\lambda_t\) and an error \(\varepsilon_{it}\):

\[Y_{it}(0) = \alpha_i + \lambda_t + \varepsilon_{it}.\]

The common trend is \(\lambda_2 - \lambda_1 = 1\), so parallel trends holds. The unit effect is \(\alpha_i = 2 + 0.5 D_i + v_i\), where \(v_i\) is a normal error with variance 1. It rises with the dose, so treated and untreated units differ in levels. The error \(\varepsilon_{it}\) is normal with standard deviation 0.5. The effect of dose \(d\) on unit \(i\) is a common dose response \(m(d)\) scaled by a unit-specific factor \(b_i\):

\[Y_{i2}(d) - Y_{i2}(0) = b_i\, m(d), \qquad m(d) = 1.5 d - 0.3 d^2. \tag{14.8}\]

The dose response \(m(d)\) is concave. The two scenarios differ only in \(b_i\). Let \(u_i\) be a normal error with standard deviation 0.2. In scenario A, \(b_i = 1 + u_i\), independent of the dose. In scenario B, \(b_i = 1 + 0.5(D_i - 1.5) + u_i\) for treated units, so units with larger gains take larger doses.

The truth follows from Equation 14.8. In both scenarios \(E(b_i \mid D_i > 0) = 1\), so \(ATE(d) = m(d)\). In scenario A, \(ATT(d \mid d) = m(d)\) as well. In scenario B, \(ATT(d \mid d) = [1 + 0.5(d - 1.5)]\, m(d)\). It lies below \(m(d)\) for doses under 1.5 and above it for doses over 1.5.

We estimate the curve in Equation 14.4 by regressing \(\Delta Y_i\) on a cubic polynomial in \(D_i\) among the treated, and subtracting the mean of \(\Delta Y_i\) among the untreated. In scenario B the true \(ATT(d \mid d)\) is a cubic, so the polynomial can fit it exactly.

Code 
# simulated from the DGP above by data/gen_did_dose_sim.R
sim = CSV.read("data/did_dose_sim.csv", DataFrame)
m(d)  = 1.5d - 0.3d^2
mp(d) = 1.5 - 0.6d          # derivative of m

function dose_curve(dt)
    w = unstack(dt, [:id, :d], :t, :y; renamecols = t -> "y$t")
    w.dy = w.y2 .- w.y1
    tr = w[w.d .> 0, :]
    X = hcat(ones(nrow(tr)), tr.d, tr.d .^ 2, tr.d .^ 3)
    base = mean(w.dy[w.d .== 0])
    (beta = X \ tr.dy, base = base, dose = tr.d, att = mean(tr.dy) - base)
end
fits = Dict(sc => dose_curve(sim[sim.scenario .== sc, :]) for sc in ["A", "B"])

grid = collect(0.5:0.05:2.5)
kappa = Dict("A" => 0.0, "B" => 0.5)

14.3 Staggered adoption

Now units enter treatment at different times, as in the ETWFE section of the DiD chapter. Periods run \(t = 1, \dots, T\), and the first period in which any unit is treated is \(q\). A cohort \(g \in \{q, \dots, T\}\) is the set of units first treated in period \(g\). Let \(D_{ig} = 1\) if unit \(i\) is in cohort \(g\), and \(D_{ig} = 0\) otherwise. Never-treated units have \(D_{ig} = 0\) for every \(g\). The vector \(\mathbf D_i = (D_{iq}, \dots, D_{iT})\) collects unit \(i\)’s cohort dummies. For each period \(s\), the time dummy \(fs_t\) equals 1 if \(t = s\) and 0 otherwise.

The treatment \(\mathbf W_{it}\) is a \(1 \times J\) row vector, where \(J\) is the number of treatment variables. A single dose has \(J = 1\). A dose and its square has \(J = 2\). A set of mutually exclusive treatment levels has \(J\) equal to the number of levels. Every unit has \(\mathbf W_{it} = \mathbf 0\) before its entry period, and \(\mathbf W_i\) stacks unit \(i\)’s rows over all \(T\) periods. We write \(\mathbf w\) for a possible value of \(\mathbf W_{it}\). The dose may change after entry. In the Walmart data it is the number of stores open.

Wooldridge (2026) writes potential outcomes as linear in the treatment, with slopes that vary by unit and period. Let \(\mathbf B_{it}\) be a \(J \times 1\) vector of random slopes, whose \(j\)-th element is unit \(i\)’s gain in period \(t\) from one unit of treatment variable \(j\). Let \(\gamma_t\) be a time effect, \(C_i\) an unobserved unit effect, and \(U_{it}\) an idiosyncratic error. The potential outcome at treatment value \(\mathbf w\) is

\[Y_{it}(\mathbf w) = \mathbf w\, \mathbf B_{it} + \gamma_t + C_i + U_{it}. \tag{14.9}\]

The additive \(\gamma_t + C_i\) is parallel trends. Nothing restricts \(\mathbf B_{it}\). The estimand is its mean for each cohort and period:

\[\boldsymbol\delta_{gs} = E(\mathbf B_{is} \mid D_{ig} = 1), \qquad s \ge g. \tag{14.10}\]

In words, \(\boldsymbol\delta_{gs}\) is the average effect of one more unit of each treatment variable for cohort \(g\) in period \(s\). With a binary treatment it is the cohort-period ATT of ETWFE.

Wooldridge assumes no anticipation and strict exogeneity. Strict exogeneity says the error \(U_{it}\) has mean zero given the whole treatment path \(\mathbf W_i\), the unit effect \(C_i\) and the slopes \(\mathbf B_{it}\). He needs one more assumption, which has no counterpart in the binary case. He calls it limited selection:

\[E(\mathbf B_{it} \mid \mathbf W_i) = E(\mathbf B_{it} \mid \mathbf D_i). \tag{14.11}\]

Once we know when a unit entered, its dose path carries no information about its average gain. With a binary treatment the entry date fixes the whole treatment path, so Equation 14.11 holds automatically. With two periods and a scalar dose it is the strong parallel trends of Equation 14.6 in its linear form.

Let \(A_i = E(C_i \mid \mathbf W_i)\) be the mean of the unit effect given the treatment path. Under these assumptions,

\[E(Y_{it} \mid \mathbf W_i) = \sum_{g=q}^{T} \sum_{s=g}^{T} (D_{ig}\, fs_t\, \mathbf W_{it})\, \boldsymbol\delta_{gs} + \gamma_t + A_i. \tag{14.12}\]

\(A_i\) is a unit effect. So a two-way fixed effects (TWFE) regression of \(Y_{it}\) on the interactions \(D_{ig}\, fs_t\, \mathbf W_{it}\), with unit and time effects, estimates every \(\boldsymbol\delta_{gs}\). This is ETWFE with the treatment indicator replaced by the dose. Standard errors are clustered by unit.

There are many cohort-period slopes, so we aggregate them. Let \(e\) be the exposure time, the number of periods since entry, and let \(N_g\) be the number of units in cohort \(g\). Let \(\hat{\boldsymbol\delta}_{gs}\) be the TWFE estimate of \(\boldsymbol\delta_{gs}\). The estimated effect at exposure \(e\), written \(\hat{\boldsymbol\tau}_{e}\), averages over the cohorts observed that long, with weights proportional to cohort size:

\[\hat{\boldsymbol\tau}_{e} = \sum_{g=q}^{T-e} \frac{N_g}{N_q + \dots + N_{T-e}}\, \hat{\boldsymbol\delta}_{g,g+e}. \tag{14.13}\]

The overall effect weights every cohort-period slope by \(N_g\). In a balanced panel it is the average slope over all treated unit-periods.

Pre-trends can be tested in two ways. A lead is a dummy \(D_{ig}\, fs_t\) for a period \(s\) before cohort \(g\) enters. The first test fits TWFE on the untreated observations only, the unit-periods with \(\mathbf W_{it} = \mathbf 0\), with leads for \(s \le g - 2\), so that the period just before entry is the base. It then tests whether the leads are zero. The second test adds the same leads to Equation 14.12. With a binary treatment the two give identical estimates. With a dose they differ. When the leads show a trend, Wooldridge adds cohort-specific linear trends to Equation 14.12. For each cohort \(g\), the regressor \(D_{ig} \cdot t\) is a linear time trend for that cohort only. The assumption then becomes that each cohort’s gap from the never-treated units would have kept growing along the same line after entry.

14.4 Wooldridge’s regression and the CGBS estimator

The two approaches target the same objects. Under parallel trends and strong parallel trends, each identifies the average effect of the dose for every cohort and period. They differ in how they estimate it and in what they can handle.

The first difference is the comparison group. Wooldridge runs one TWFE regression in levels on all periods, Equation 14.12. Every untreated observation serves as a control: the never-treated units, the not-yet-treated units, and the pre-entry periods of every cohort. In the binary case this regression is the imputation estimator of Borusyak, Jaravel, and Spiess (2024). Callaway, Goodman-Bacon, and Sant’Anna (2024) build on Callaway and Sant’Anna (2021) instead. For each cohort and period they compare the cohort with the never-treated or the not-yet-treated units, using the long difference from the period just before entry, and fit the dose response in that comparison. The regression uses more data, and it is more efficient when the errors are homoskedastic and serially uncorrelated. In exchange it needs parallel trends in every pre-entry period. The differencing approach needs it only from the base period on.

The second difference is the shape of the dose response. In Wooldridge’s regression, the shape comes from the functions of the dose placed in \(\mathbf W_{it}\): the dose itself, its powers, or dummies for dose levels. The estimand is the average slope \(\boldsymbol\delta_{gs}\). CGBS fit a B-spline in the dose within each cohort and period, or a nonparametric curve whose complexity is chosen from the data. They report the curve \(ATT(d \mid d)\) and its derivative. The two also treat a dose near zero differently. With two periods, Wooldridge’s regression has no separate intercept for the treated units, so its fitted effect goes to zero as the dose goes to zero. The CGBS fit gives the treated units their own intercept, so the effect can jump at any positive dose. The two coincide once a dummy for a positive dose is added to \(\mathbf W_{it}\). The estimator in our simulation follows CGBS.

The third difference is scope. The table compares Wooldridge’s regression with the R package contdid, version 0.1.1, which implements CGBS.

14.5 Walmart and county retail employment

Wooldridge (2026) applies the method to the county panel of Brown and Butts (2025). It covers 1,288 counties from 1977 to 1999. No county had a Walmart before 1986. Stores then opened in 14 cohorts, from 1986 to 1999, and 395 counties never got one. The treatment is a single dose, so \(J = 1\) and we write it without bold: \(W_{it}\) is the number of Walmarts open in county \(i\) in year \(t\). The outcome is log retail employment.

That gives 105 slopes. We aggregate them with Equation 14.13. Each aggregate is a linear combination of the slopes with fixed weights, so its standard error comes from the clustered covariance matrix. This matches Stata’s margins in Wooldridge’s code.

Code 
Ng = Dict(g => sum((wm.year .== 1999) .& (wm.cohort .== g)) for g in cohorts)

function aggregate_slopes(fit)
    pos = Dict(n => i for (i, n) in enumerate(coefnames(fit)))
    b = coef(fit); V = vcov(fit)
    lincomb(a) = (dot(a, b), sqrt(a' * V * a))
    out = map(0:13) do e
        gs = [g for g in cohorts if g + e <= 1999]
        a = zeros(length(b))
        for g in gs
            a[pos["w_$(g)_$(g + e)"]] = Ng[g] / sum(Ng[h] for h in gs)
        end
        lincomb(a)
    end
    a = zeros(length(b))
    for (g, s) in cells
        a[pos["w_$(g)_$(s)"]] = Ng[g]
    end
    push!(out, lincomb(a ./ sum(a)))
end
agg_het = aggregate_slopes(fit_het);

Before reading the effects, we check pre-trends. Following the first test above, we fit TWFE on the untreated county-years with a lead for each cohort and each year at least two years before entry. We then average the leads by years until entry, with the cohort weights of Equation 14.13.

Code 
un = wm[wm.n_open .== 0, [:fips, :year, :cohort, :log_retail_emp]]

    un[!, c] = Float64.((un.cohort .== g) .& (un.year .== s))

fit_pre = feols(un, term(:log_retail_emp) ~ sum(term.(lcols)) + fe(:fips) + fe(:year),

14.6 Where it can go wrong

Everything above rests on limited selection. In the Walmart data the dose changes after entry, so Equation 14.11 requires that the number of stores a county ends up with says nothing about its gain from them. A county where the first store did well is a likely site for a second and a third. If so, high-count counties have larger slopes, and the estimates mix the causal response with selection on gains, as in scenario B of the simulation. Store openings that respond to past retail growth also violate strict exogeneity. The model is also linear in the count, so the second store has the same effect as the first. Adding \(W_{it}^2\), or the indicator \(1[W_{it} \ge 2]\) that equals 1 when the county has two or more stores, to \(\mathbf W_{it}\) checks this.

The trend-adjusted estimates extrapolate a straight line for up to 13 years after entry. The pre-period leads support a straight line before entry. Nothing in the data can check whether it continues after entry. There is a second issue. Equation 14.12 has no separate intercept for each cohort and year after entry, and every treated county has at least one store. So the trend \(D_{ig} \cdot t\) is fit from post-entry years as well as from the years before. If the effect grows with exposure, part of that growth can load onto the trend. Wolfers (2006) showed this with unit-specific trends for divorce laws. It is one reason the trend estimates are so much smaller in the middle years.

A sensitivity analysis in the style of Rambachan and Roth (2023) suits this case. It bounds how far the post-entry path may depart from the extrapolated trend, and reports the effect as a function of that bound.
